What my work changed

Every CFD study starts with a mesh, and building one around a complex geometry is still slow and largely manual: NASA's CFD Vision 2030 study names it one of the main bottlenecks of the whole workflow.1

During my Ph.D. at Politecnico di Milano (2022–2026) I worked on it at two levels. First the method: cutMesh, a cut-cell library that meshes any closed shape on a Cartesian grid automatically, tested on 9 benchmark geometries and a standard OpenFOAM case. Then a real application: a workflow that turns raw LiDAR scans of a Milan district into wind and thermal-comfort simulations, which match the measured mean wind speed within about 5%.

About

From aircraft to cities, and beyond

I'm Italian and studied aerospace engineering at Politecnico di Milano, then earned my Ph.D. there in Energy and Nuclear Science and Technology, with the Department of Aerospace Science and Technology. Aircraft taught me the fundamentals: fluid dynamics, numerical methods and a healthy respect for validation. I have worked in CFD for 5+ years, since my M.S. thesis.

During my Ph.D. I applied those tools to the wind, heat and comfort of real urban districts, building the whole workflow myself: classifying LiDAR point clouds, rebuilding 1,259 buildings in 3D, meshing them and running large OpenFOAM simulations on a cluster, which matched the measured mean wind speed within about 5%. Where tools were missing, I wrote them, like cutMesh, a cut-cell library that meshes any closed shape automatically.

As a visiting researcher at the Université libre de Bruxelles, I built the point-cloud classifier now used in the Milan workflow and fine-tuned the RandLA-Net network on GPU.

None of this is specific to cities: turbulence, heat transfer, meshing of complex geometry, validation and parallel computing are what energy, aerospace and process engineering rely on. That's where I want to apply them next.

Today I live in Houston.
